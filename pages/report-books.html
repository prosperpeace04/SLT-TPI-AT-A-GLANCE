EXPERIMENT 1

Qualitative Tests for Carbohydrates

▼
Aim

To carry out qualitative tests for the identification of carbohydrates (reducing sugars, non-reducing sugars, starch and polysaccharides) using standard chemical tests.

Introduction

Carbohydrates are the most abundant biomolecules on earth. They serve as energy sources, structural components, and are involved in cell recognition. Qualitative tests such as Benedict's test, Fehling's test, Iodine test and Molisch's test are used to identify the presence and type of carbohydrates in a sample.

Benedict's Test: Used to detect reducing sugars. Reducing sugars (glucose, maltose, lactose) contain free aldehyde or ketone groups that can donate electrons to Cu²⁺ in Benedict's reagent, reducing it to Cu₂O (brick-red precipitate).

Iodine Test: Used to detect starch. Iodine forms a dark blue-black complex with starch (amylose component). The colour disappears on heating and reappears on cooling.

Molisch's Test: A general test for carbohydrates. Concentrated H₂SO₄ dehydrates carbohydrates to form furfural (from pentoses) or hydroxymethylfurfural (from hexoses), which reacts with α-naphthol to form a purple ring at the interface.

Materials and Apparatus
  • Test tubes and test tube rack
  • Water bath
  • Benedict's reagent, Fehling's solution A and B
  • Iodine solution (potassium iodide/iodine)
  • Molisch's reagent (α-naphthol in ethanol)
  • Concentrated sulphuric acid
  • Dropper and pipette
  • Known carbohydrate solutions: glucose, sucrose, starch, lactose
Procedure

Benedict's Test: Add 2 cm³ of Benedict's reagent to 1 cm³ of test solution. Heat in water bath for 5 minutes. Observe for colour change. A brick-red precipitate indicates a reducing sugar.

Iodine Test: Add 2–3 drops of iodine solution to 2 cm³ of test solution. Observe for blue-black colour indicating starch.

Molisch's Test: Add 2 drops of Molisch's reagent to 2 cm³ of test solution. Mix gently. Carefully add 2 cm³ of concentrated H₂SO₄ down the side of the tube. Observe for purple ring at the interface, indicating a carbohydrate.

Discussion

Describe and explain all observations. Discuss the chemical basis of each positive reaction. Account for any negative results. Compare results for different carbohydrate samples tested.

Conclusion

Summarize what carbohydrates were identified in each sample. State whether the aim of the experiment was achieved.

EXPERIMENT 2

Qualitative Tests for Proteins

▼
Aim

To carry out qualitative tests for the identification and characterization of proteins using Biuret test, Ninhydrin test, and Millon's test.

Introduction

Proteins are complex nitrogenous compounds made of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. They serve as enzymes, structural proteins, transport proteins, antibodies and hormones.

Biuret Test: Used to detect the presence of peptide bonds (-CO-NH-) in proteins. In alkaline solution, copper ions form a violet/purple complex with two or more peptide bonds.

Ninhydrin Test: Ninhydrin reacts with α-amino acids to produce a purple-blue colour (Ruhemann's purple). Proline and hydroxyproline give a yellow colour instead.

Millon's Test: Used to detect proteins containing tyrosine (a phenolic amino acid). Millon's reagent (mercuric nitrate in nitric acid) reacts with phenolic compounds to form a red precipitate.

Xanthoproteic Test: Concentrated HNO₃ nitrates aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tryptophan, tyrosine) to produce a yellow colour that turns orange-yellow in alkali.

Materials and Apparatus
  • Test tubes, test tube rack, water bath
  • Biuret reagent (NaOH + CuSO₄)
  • Ninhydrin solution (0.2% in acetone or ethanol)
  • Millon's reagent
  • Concentrated nitric acid and sodium hydroxide
  • Protein solutions: albumin (egg white), gelatin, casein
Procedure

Biuret Test: Add 1 cm³ of 10% NaOH to 2 cm³ of protein solution. Add 2–3 drops of 1% CuSO₄ solution. Mix well. Observe colour — violet/purple indicates peptide bonds (protein present).

Ninhydrin Test: Add 3 drops of ninhydrin solution to 1 cm³ of protein solution. Heat in water bath for 5 minutes. Purple-blue colour indicates amino acids or proteins.

Xanthoproteic Test: Add 1 cm³ of concentrated HNO₃ to 1 cm³ of protein solution. Heat gently. Cool. Add dilute NaOH. Yellow turning orange-yellow indicates aromatic amino acids.

Discussion

Explain the chemical basis of each positive reaction. Discuss which amino acids are detected by each test. Relate the results to the protein samples tested.

Conclusion

State which tests gave positive results for each protein sample. Confirm whether the aim was achieved.

EXPERIMENT 3

Qualitative Tests for Lipids

▼
Aim

To carry out qualitative tests for the identification of lipids using the emulsification test, solubility test, and Sudan III / Sudan IV staining.

Introduction

Lipids are a diverse group of hydrophobic biomolecules including fats, oils, phospholipids, steroids and waxes. They serve as energy reserves, membrane components, insulation and signalling molecules. Since lipids are non-polar, they are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents.

Emulsification Test: Lipids form a white emulsion when ethanol extracts are added to water. This is due to the formation of small lipid droplets suspended in water.

Sudan III/IV Staining: Sudan dyes are hydrophobic and preferentially dissolve in lipid droplets, staining them red-orange (Sudan III) or red (Sudan IV).

Materials and Apparatus
  • Test tubes and test tube rack
  • Ethanol (absolute)
  • Sudan III or Sudan IV stain
  • Lipid samples: cooking oil, butter, lard
  • Brown paper (grease spot test)
  • Dropper and pipette
Procedure

Emulsification Test: Dissolve a few drops of cooking oil in 2 cm³ of ethanol. Add this mixture to 3 cm³ of water. Shake vigorously. A milky-white emulsion confirms the presence of lipid.

Sudan III Stain: Place a drop of oil on a glass slide. Add a drop of Sudan III solution. Observe under microscope or with naked eye. Orange-red coloration indicates lipid.

Grease Spot Test: Rub a drop of the sample on brown paper. Hold up to light after allowing to dry. A translucent spot indicates lipid (greasy residue).

Results Table
TestObservationInference
EmulsificationWhite/milky emulsionLipid present
Sudan III StainOrange-red colorationLipid present
Grease SpotTranslucent spot on paperLipid present
Discussion

Explain each test on the basis of the hydrophobic nature of lipids. Discuss which lipid samples gave the most distinct results. Explain why lipids dissolve readily in ethanol but not in water.

Conclusion

Confirm the presence of lipids in each sample tested. State whether the aim was achieved.

EXPERIMENT 4

Determination of Enzyme Activity (Amylase)

▼
Aim

To determine the activity of amylase enzyme on starch and the effect of temperature on enzyme activity.

Introduction

Enzymes are biological catalysts — proteins that speed up chemical reactions in living organisms without being consumed. Amylase is an enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of starch into simpler sugars (maltose and glucose).

Enzyme activity is affected by temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and inhibitors. At optimal temperature, enzyme activity is maximum. Above this temperature, enzymes are denatured and activity falls sharply. The rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction can be monitored by the rate of substrate disappearance or product formation.

Materials and Apparatus
  • 1% starch solution
  • Salivary amylase (fresh saliva diluted 1:10) or commercial amylase
  • Iodine solution
  • Benedict's reagent
  • Water baths at different temperatures: 20°C, 37°C, 60°C, 80°C
  • Test tubes, stopwatch, dropper
  • Spotting tile
Procedure

Place 5 cm³ of starch solution and 1 cm³ of amylase solution in test tubes. Incubate at different temperatures (20°C, 37°C, 60°C, 80°C) for 10 minutes. Take samples at 2-minute intervals. Test each sample with iodine on a spotting tile. Record the time taken for the iodine test to give no blue-black colour (complete hydrolysis of starch).

Discuss how temperature affects enzyme activity. Explain the concept of optimum temperature. Account for the loss of activity at high temperatures (denaturation). Draw and discuss a graph of enzyme activity vs temperature.

EXPERIMENT 1

Preparation and Observation of Mitosis in Onion Root Tips

▼
Aim

To prepare and observe the stages of mitosis (cell division) in onion root tip cells using the squash technique and acetocarmine staining.

Introduction

Mitosis is a type of cell division in which one parent cell divides to produce two genetically identical daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent. Mitosis is essential for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction in organisms.

The stages of mitosis are: Interphase (preparation), Prophase (chromosomes condense), Metaphase (chromosomes align at equatorial plate), Anaphase (sister chromatids separate), and Telophase (cell divides). Plant root tips are ideal for observing mitosis because cells in the meristematic zone are actively dividing.

Materials and Apparatus
  • Fresh onion (Allium cepa) root tips (grown in water for 2–3 days)
  • Acetocarmine or Feulgen stain
  • 1 N hydrochloric acid
  • Slides and cover slips
  • Forceps, scalpel, water bath
  • Compound microscope
  • Aceto-orcein stain (alternative)
Procedure

Cut off 1–2 cm of growing root tips. Place in 1 N HCl at 60°C for 5–8 minutes (maceration). Transfer to acetocarmine stain and heat gently for 5 minutes. Place a root tip on a clean slide. Add a drop of acetocarmine. Cover with a cover slip. Press firmly with a pencil eraser (squash technique) to spread cells. Observe under the microscope at ×10 then ×40.

Results — Stages to Look For
StageMicroscopic Appearance
InterphaseNucleus visible, chromatin dispersed (resting stage)
ProphaseChromosomes condense and become visible, nuclear envelope breaks down
MetaphaseChromosomes aligned at the cell's equatorial plate (metaphase plate)
AnaphaseSister chromatids pulled to opposite poles of the cell
TelophaseTwo new nuclei form, cell plate appears between daughter cells

Draw diagrams of each stage observed. Label the chromosomes, cell wall, and spindle fibres where visible.

Discussion

Describe the stages of mitosis observed. Explain the significance of each stage. Discuss why the meristematic zone of the root tip is used. Discuss any difficulties encountered in preparing the slide.

Conclusion

Confirm whether the stages of mitosis were successfully observed. State the significance of mitosis in the life of an organism.

EXPERIMENT 2

Monohybrid Cross — Simulation with Beads

▼
Aim

To simulate a monohybrid cross and verify Mendel's Law of Segregation using coloured beads or coins to represent alleles.

Introduction

Gregor Mendel's Law of Segregation states that during the formation of gametes, the two alleles for a gene separate so that each gamete contains only one allele. When these gametes fuse at fertilization, the offspring receives one allele from each parent.

In a monohybrid cross between two heterozygous parents (Aa × Aa), the expected phenotypic ratio in the offspring is 3:1 (dominant to recessive), and the genotypic ratio is 1:2:1 (AA:Aa:aa).

Let A = dominant allele (e.g. tall); a = recessive allele (e.g. dwarf).

Materials
  • Two bags containing coloured beads (e.g. 50 red beads = A, 50 white beads = a) — one bag per parent
  • Table for recording results
Procedure

Label two bags as "Parent 1" and "Parent 2." Place 50 red (A) and 50 white (a) beads in each bag. Without looking, randomly pick one bead from each bag simultaneously. Record the combination obtained. Return the beads and repeat 100 times. Count and tabulate the results: AA, Aa, aA, aa.

Expected Results
CombinationGenotypePhenotypeExpected Frequency (out of 100)Observed Frequency
Red + RedAADominant (Tall)25[Record]
Red + White / White + RedAa / aADominant (Tall)50[Record]
White + WhiteaaRecessive (Dwarf)25[Record]
Discussion

Compare observed ratios to the expected 1:2:1 genotypic and 3:1 phenotypic ratios. Discuss any deviations from expected ratios and explain using probability theory. Relate the simulation to Mendel's Law of Segregation. Explain why the ratio might not be exactly 3:1 in a small sample.

Conclusion

State the genotypic and phenotypic ratios obtained. Confirm whether the results support Mendel's Law of Segregation.

EXPERIMENT 3

Preparation and Observation of Chromosomes (Karyotyping)

▼
Aim

To prepare a karyotype by arranging photographs or diagrams of human chromosomes in homologous pairs and interpret the karyotype for chromosomal abnormalities.

Introduction

A karyotype is the complete set of chromosomes in a cell, arranged in homologous pairs in order of decreasing size. The normal human karyotype contains 46 chromosomes (23 pairs), including 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes (XX in females, XY in males). Karyotyping is used to detect chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Turner syndrome (45,X), and Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY).

Materials
  • Printed photographs or drawings of human chromosomes (from a standard karyotype sheet)
  • Scissors and glue or tape
  • Ruler for measuring chromosome lengths
  • Karyotype analysis sheet
Procedure

Cut out each chromosome from the photograph. Arrange the chromosomes in homologous pairs from largest (pair 1) to smallest (pair 22), plus the sex chromosomes. Paste them in order on the karyotype sheet. Determine the sex of the individual from the sex chromosomes. Examine for any chromosomal abnormalities (extra or missing chromosomes).

Results

Completed karyotype chart showing all 23 pairs of chromosomes arranged by size. Record: Total number of chromosomes observed, sex of the individual (XX or XY), any chromosomal abnormalities detected.

Discussion

Describe the karyotype prepared. Identify the sex of the individual. Discuss any abnormalities detected and their clinical significance. Explain the process of meiosis and how non-disjunction leads to chromosomal abnormalities.

Conclusion

State the number of chromosomes counted, the sex determined, and whether any abnormality was detected. Confirm whether the aim was achieved.

EXPERIMENT 1

Study of an Ecosystem — Soil Sampling and Analysis

▼
Aim

To collect soil samples from different habitats, analyze their physical and chemical properties, and relate the properties to the organisms found in that habitat.

Introduction

Soil is a complex mixture of inorganic minerals, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms. It forms the foundation of terrestrial ecosystems. The physical properties of soil (texture, porosity, water content) and chemical properties (pH, mineral content, organic matter) determine the types of organisms that can live in and on the soil.

Soil pH affects nutrient availability. Most plants grow best in slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 6.0–7.0). Soil texture (proportion of sand, silt and clay) affects drainage and aeration. Sandy soils drain quickly but hold few nutrients; clay soils hold water and nutrients but have poor drainage.

Materials and Apparatus
  • Soil samples from at least two different habitats (e.g. garden soil, forest soil, waterlogged area)
  • pH meter or universal indicator paper
  • Drying oven and weighing balance
  • Filter paper, funnel, beaker
  • Measuring cylinder
  • Hand lens or magnifying glass
  • Petri dishes
Procedure

Water Content: Weigh a known mass of fresh soil. Dry in oven at 105°C for 24 hours. Reweigh. Calculate percentage water content: [(fresh weight − dry weight) / fresh weight] × 100.

Soil pH: Mix 10 g of soil with 25 cm³ of distilled water. Stir and allow to settle. Measure pH of the supernatant.

Soil Texture: Rub a moist sample between fingers. Sandy soil feels gritty; silt feels silky; clay feels sticky when wet and hard when dry.

Percolation Rate: Fill a funnel with a measured amount of soil. Pour 100 cm³ of water slowly. Measure the volume and time for water to drain through.

EXPERIMENT 2

Population Estimation Using the Quadrat Method

▼
Aim

To estimate the population density and distribution of plant species in a given area using the quadrat method.

Introduction

A quadrat is a square frame of a known size used to sample the number and type of organisms in a defined area of a habitat. By counting the organisms in multiple quadrats placed randomly, ecologists can estimate the total population in a larger area. The quadrat method is particularly useful for slow-moving or sessile organisms such as plants, barnacles, and lichens.

Population density = Total number of individuals counted / Total area sampled. Frequency = Number of quadrats in which the species occurs / Total number of quadrats placed × 100%.

Materials and Apparatus
  • Quadrat frame (0.5 m × 0.5 m or 1 m × 1 m) made of wire or string
  • Random number table or measuring tape
  • Tally counter and data recording sheet
  • Identification keys for plant species (if available)
Procedure

Select a study area (e.g. school compound lawn or nearby field). Using random number tables or stepping method, place the quadrat at random positions in the study area. Within each quadrat, count the number of each plant species present. Repeat for at least 10 quadrats. Calculate population density and frequency for each species.

EXPERIMENT 3

Construction and Analysis of a Food Chain and Food Web

▼
Aim

To construct a food chain and food web from collected field data and analyze the flow of energy through an ecosystem.

Introduction

A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms through which nutrients and energy pass as one organism eats another. A food web is a network of interconnected food chains representing the feeding relationships in an ecosystem. Energy flows unidirectionally through the ecosystem — from producers (plants) to primary consumers (herbivores) to secondary consumers (carnivores) to tertiary consumers.

Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level (10% rule). This is because energy is lost as heat through metabolism, movement, and respiration. Pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy can be used to represent trophic levels.

Types of organisms: Producers (autotrophs), Primary consumers (herbivores), Secondary consumers (carnivores), Tertiary consumers (top carnivores), Decomposers (bacteria, fungi).

Materials
  • Field notebook with observations of organisms in a study area
  • Pencil, ruler, and drawing paper
  • Reference books on local flora and fauna
Procedure

Identify the major producers, consumers and decomposers in a study area from field observations. List at least 8–10 organisms. Construct at least 3 food chains from the data. Connect the food chains into a food web by drawing arrows from food source to consumer. Construct a pyramid of numbers based on the data collected.

Sample Food Chains to Draw (from a forest ecosystem)
Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk Leaf litter → Earthworm → Thrush → Sparrowhawk Phytoplankton → Zooplankton → Small fish → Large fish → Osprey
Results

Draw and label your food web clearly with arrows showing energy flow direction. Construct a pyramid of numbers showing the organisms at each trophic level. Label: Producers, Primary consumers, Secondary consumers, Tertiary consumers, Decomposers.

Discussion

Discuss the importance of producers in the ecosystem. Explain why the energy pyramid is broad at the base and narrow at the top. Discuss the role of decomposers in nutrient cycling. Explain what would happen if one organism in the food web was removed (keystone species concept). Relate the 10% energy transfer rule to the length of food chains.

Conclusion

Summarize the trophic structure of the ecosystem studied. State the significance of food webs in ecological studies. Confirm whether the aim was achieved.
